How VCs Evaluate a Startup

The Four Things Every Investor Is Actually Looking At

Ask ten different investors what they look for in a startup and you’ll get ten slightly different answers. But underneath the differences, almost every VC is evaluating the same four things: the team, the market, the product, and the traction. Understanding these gives you a real framework for looking at any startup — and it’s the same framework applied to the real examples on the Real-World Cases page.

The Team

Early-stage investing is really a bet on people before it’s a bet on a product, because the product will almost certainly change. Investors look for founders who deeply understand the problem they’re solving, usually because they’ve lived it themselves, and who can show they’re capable of learning fast and adapting when their first idea doesn’t work. A strong team doesn’t guarantee success, but a weak one is often enough to pass on an otherwise interesting idea.

The Market

A great team building a product for a market that’s too small will still struggle to build a venture-scale company. Investors ask how big the opportunity actually is, and — just as importantly — how it’s likely to grow. A market that’s small today but expanding quickly is often more attractive than one that’s already large but stagnant, because early-stage VCs are betting on where things are headed, not where they are.

The Product

At the early stage, the product doesn’t need to be finished — it needs to show that the team can actually build something people want. Investors look at how quickly a team ships, how they respond to user feedback, and whether the product solves the problem in a way that’s meaningfully better than existing alternatives, not just marginally different.

Traction

Traction is the evidence that the first three things are actually working. That can look like user growth, revenue, retention, or even just a waitlist that’s growing on its own. Investors don’t expect huge numbers this early, but they do look for a trend line moving in the right direction, and evidence that people are engaging with the product because they genuinely want to, not because someone asked them to try it out.

Putting It Together

None of these four factors work in isolation — a strong team can sometimes make up for early weaknesses elsewhere, and strong traction can make investors overlook gaps in the pitch. The Real-World Cases page puts this exact framework to the test against three real, documented VC decisions.

Investor Checklist

  • Team: who is building this?
  • Market: how big could it get?
  • Product: is it actually good?
  • Traction: is it working?
